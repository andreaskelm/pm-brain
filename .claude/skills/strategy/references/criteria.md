# Strategy Criteria

Flags only, on purpose: a strategy is judged by whether it makes real choices that hold up, not by a score.

## Red flags

- **Fluff** — buzzwords doing the work of thought: "customer-centric, data-driven, AI-first". Delete the adjectives and nothing is left.
- **No hard problem named** — the doc never says what obstacle it addresses; symptoms ("engagement is down") stand in for a diagnosis
- **Goals presented as strategy** — "our strategy is to reach $50M ARR" or "become the market leader", with no how
- **Laundry list** — 8+ priorities, unranked, more than the team could resource; or objectives that contradict each other (cut cost AND go premium AND move faster)
- **No non-goals, or fake ones** — nothing excluded, or exclusions nobody wanted ("we won't ship buggy features")
- **Function-shaped pillars** — "Mobile", "Platform", "Growth": org-chart boxes, not choices
- **Generic how-to-win** — "best-in-class UX", "great service": a competitor could put their logo on it
- **Capability fantasy** — how-to-win depends on something the team doesn't have and has no plan to build or buy
- **Incoherent actions** — initiatives that don't reinforce each other, or work against the policy
- **Systems pulling the other way** — "win enterprise" while metrics and incentives still reward SMB volume
- **Contradicts company strategy, silently** — a team strategy at odds with the one above it, and the doc doesn't say so
- **Nothing stops** — no in-flight project or recurring request gets killed or delayed by it

## Green flags

- A diagnosis that explains *why* the problem exists, in plain words, and points toward a response
- The uncomfortable truth is in the doc (the competitor that's winning, the segment that's leaving, the bet that failed)
- Every "we will" has a "we won't" that someone in the org would argue against
- 3–5 pillars at most, each with evidence and its strength named
- A how-to-win tied to an advantage that's hard to copy: data, distribution, a customer relationship, a capability
- Actions where you can say how A makes B work better
- Assumptions listed as "what would have to be true", with a test for the shakiest one
- A guardrail per pillar: what must not get worse
- A reopen trigger: the signal that would make you revisit the diagnosis
- Fits on 1–2 pages before the appendices

## Rewrite examples

**Diagnosis**
- Before: "The market is increasingly competitive and we need to accelerate growth."
- After: "Mid-market accounts churn around month 4 (38% of churn, 2024 exit data) because they outgrow our permissions model. Competitors with admin tooling win the renewal. Our problem isn't acquisition; it's that we're a starter tool customers graduate from."
- Why: names the obstacle, the cause and the evidence, and it already points at a response.

**Guiding policy**
- Before: "Deliver an exceptional, customer-centric experience across all segments."
- After: "Become the tool mid-market teams don't outgrow: invest in admin, permissions and audit for 50–500 seat accounts. We will not pursue a self-serve SMB motion or a freemium tier this year, even though both are requested."
- Why: a choice with an opposite someone wants, and it follows from the diagnosis.

**Non-goal**
- Before: "Non-goals: low-value features, unnecessary complexity."
- After: "Non-goal: native mobile app. Why not now: under 4% of sessions are mobile and the churn problem is admin, not access. What we stop: the mobile discovery track and the agency's Q3 prototype. Revisit if mobile sessions pass 15%."
- Why: something real gets stopped, with a reason and a reopen trigger.
