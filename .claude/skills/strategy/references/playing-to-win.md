# Playing to Win: the Strategic Choice Cascade

From A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin, *Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works* (2013). Their definition: "Strategy is an integrated set of choices that uniquely positions the firm in its industry so as to create sustainable advantage and superior value relative to the competition."

Use it when the work is *making* choices: which segment, which market, which positioning, which of two or three directions. It's not for ranking features, and it's overkill when the answers are already clear and stable.

## The five choices

Answer them in order, then loop back. A choice lower down regularly forces you to revise one higher up.

| Choice | Question | Olay example | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Winning aspiration** | What does winning mean, in customer terms? | Help women look and feel beautiful at every age | Does it force choices? Would missing it mean we failed? |
| **2. Where to play** | Which segments, products, geographies, channels, and which NOT? | Premium anti-aging skincare, women 25–49, mass retail, developed markets first. Not budget, not medical channels, not teens | Is it clear what we're saying no to? |
| **3. How to win** | Why will customers in that field choose us over the alternatives? | Science-backed formulations at accessible prices: premium benefits in the mass channel | Could a competitor claim the same sentence? Can we sustain it? |
| **4. Core capabilities** | What must we be great at to win that way? | R&D, clinical testing, manufacturing scale, brand repositioning, retail relationships | Does each one directly enable how-to-win? What's the gap? |
| **5. Management systems** | What metrics, processes, resourcing and incentives keep 1–4 alive? | Clinical results, brand health, share by channel; bonuses tied to share in the target demographic | Do our incentives reward what the strategy says? |

**Where to play** is about choosing, which means choosing where not to play. Pick 2–3 playing fields at most; for a startup, often one. "We serve everyone" is the most common non-answer.

**How to win** is the heart of it. The two classic routes are cost leadership (similar value at lower cost: Walmart, IKEA) and differentiation (unique value customers pay more for: Apple, Tesla). "Win on price AND premium quality AND speed" means no choice has been made.

**Capabilities and systems** are where good strategies quietly die. "How to win: AI-powered personalization" with no ML capability is a fantasy; restate it as what you can do with what you have, and what you're building toward. "Win in enterprise" with a sales comp plan that rewards SMB deals will lose to the comp plan every time.

## Coherence checks

**Vertical (top to bottom):** does where-to-play serve the aspiration? Does how-to-win work in that field? Do the capabilities enable it? Do the systems support the capabilities?

**Horizontal (across choices):** any contradictions ("win on cost" but capabilities that need premium pricing)? Gaps (how-to-win needs a capability you can't build)? Systems pulling against the strategy?

Classic incoherent draft: aspiration "win enterprise", where "SMB", how "lowest price". Each answer sounds fine alone; together they're three different companies.

## "What would have to be true?"

The most useful move when a team is stuck between directions. Instead of arguing which option is right, take each candidate where-to-play/how-to-win pair and list what would have to be true for it to be a great choice: about customers (they value this enough to switch), competitors (they can't or won't copy it quickly), capabilities (we can build or buy it), and economics (the unit economics work at our scale). Then ask which of those conditions you're least sure of, and design the cheapest test for that one. It turns "I think" vs. "I think" into a shared list of beliefs to check, and it lets the skeptic be useful instead of obstructive.

## Nested cascades

Larger orgs run the cascade at several levels: company, business unit, product, sometimes feature. Each level makes its choices inside the constraints of the one above. For a product team, that usually means the aspiration is inherited, and your real work is where-to-play and how-to-win within it.

## Where it breaks down

It was built on P&G consumer brands with a CEO who could set all five choices at once. A product team rarely owns management systems, and often not the aspiration either. Treat choices 4 and 5 as a reality check ("can we actually do this, and will the org's incentives let us?") rather than something you get to design. And a filled-in cascade isn't the strategy; the coherence between the answers is.
