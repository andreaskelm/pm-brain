# Good Strategy / Bad Strategy: the Kernel

From Richard P. Rumelt, *Good Strategy Bad Strategy: The Difference and Why It Matters* (2011); see also his *The Crux: How Leaders Become Strategists* (2022). Rumelt's definition: "Strategy is a way through a difficulty, an approach to overcoming an obstacle, a response to a challenge."

The kernel is mainly a test. Use it to check whether something is a strategy at all, or to find out why one isn't working. You can build with it too, but it won't generate choices for you; for that, use the Playing to Win cascade and bring the answers back here.

## The three elements

All three must exist, and each must follow from the one before. If the guiding policy doesn't address the diagnosis, it doesn't matter how good either one sounds.

| Element | Core question | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Diagnosis** | What's really going on here? | "The market is competitive" | "Incumbents have network effects that make switching costly; our challenge is switching friction, not features" |
| **Guiding policy** | How will we deal with it, and what won't we do? | "Deliver great products and excellent service" | "Win underserved power users first, even if mainstream users find us harder to start with" |
| **Coherent actions** | What coordinated steps carry it out? | Ten unrelated initiatives | Five actions where each makes the next one work better |

**Diagnosis** explains *why* the challenge exists and points toward a response. The common failures: describing symptoms ("declining revenue") instead of the cause ("competitors have a better value proposition for our core segment"), stating a goal as a diagnosis ("we need to grow 50%"), and avoiding the uncomfortable truth. Test: strip the jargon. Is it still true, and does it suggest what to do?

**Guiding policy** is the guardrail, not the route. It sets direction and constraints without specifying every step, and it must say what you will NOT do. Test: would someone else know what to do, and what to refuse? Is it different from the obvious approach anyone would take?

**Coherent actions** are feasible, resourced, and mutually reinforcing. Test: for any two actions, can you explain how A makes B more effective? Remove anything that doesn't serve the guiding policy, however good it is on its own.

## Worked examples

**Walmart (1970s).** Diagnosis: small towns of 5,000–25,000 are underserved because low density makes conventional stores unprofitable and distribution to scattered locations is expensive. Guiding policy: big discount stores in small towns, made profitable through logistics and regional clustering; not urban markets, not winning on price alone. Actions: cluster stores regionally, build distribution centers per cluster, invest in logistics tech, use combined volume with suppliers, hire locally, reinvest into the next cluster. Why it coheres: clustering makes the distribution centers pay off, which keeps costs low, which funds low prices in small markets, and each cluster funds the next.

**Apple (1997).** Diagnosis: too many products diluting focus, company near bankruptcy. Guiding policy: cut to four products (consumer and pro, desktop and portable) and put everything behind them.

**Stephanie's grocery store**, a corner shop up against a 24/7 supermarket. Diagnosis: can't win on price or hours. Guiding policy: serve time-pressed professionals who value convenience over price. Actions: prepared meals, delivery to nearby offices, commute-hour opening, grab-and-go at the entrance. Every action serves the same customer.

## The four hallmarks of bad strategy

- **Fluff** — buzzwords that hide the absence of thought. "Leverage synergies to maximize stakeholder value." Detect: delete the adjectives; if nothing is left, it was fluff.
- **Failure to face the challenge** — no hard problem named, competitive threats ignored, constraints wished away. Detect: you can't find a sentence that says what obstacle this addresses.
- **Mistaking goals for strategy** — "Become the market leader", "Reach 100M users". Detect: replace the word "strategy" with "goal" and nothing changes.
- **Bad strategic objectives** — long lists, often conflicting or unfundable. "Improve quality AND cut costs AND move faster AND expand markets", or the 47-point plan. Detect: more objectives than the team could resource, and no ranking.

## Where it breaks down

The kernel tells you a diagnosis is missing; it can't tell you whether yours is right. Two smart people can diagnose the same situation differently, and that argument is the real strategy work. Also, Rumelt's examples are mostly military and retail, with one decisive actor. In a product org the guiding policy often isn't yours to set, which is why the diagnosis is the part a PM can most reliably own.

## Template

```markdown
# [Product/Team] Strategy — Kernel

## Diagnosis: what's really going on?
**The challenge:** [one specific paragraph]
**Why it exists:** [root causes, not symptoms]
**Critical factors:** [the 2–3 things that matter most]
**Evidence:** [what we know, and how strong it is: data / research / verbal / hunch]

## Guiding policy: how we'll deal with it
**Approach:** [the overall direction]
**We will NOT:** [explicit constraints someone would argue against]
**Why this follows from the diagnosis:** [the logic]

## Coherent actions
| Action | Why it serves the policy | Owner | By when |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

**How they reinforce each other:** [A enables B because…]

## Check
- [ ] Diagnosis explains why, and points to a response
- [ ] Policy addresses the diagnosis and says what we won't do
- [ ] Actions are resourced and reinforce each other; none contradict
- [ ] None of the four hallmarks present
```
